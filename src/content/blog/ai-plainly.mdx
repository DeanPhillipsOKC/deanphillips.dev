That's an AI answering a question. ChatGPT and tools like it run on a program called a **model**, and the model is what's doing the guessing. On its own, it isn't looking anything up. By the end of this post you'll know exactly what that box is showing, with no math.

## How I'm doing this

There's a learning trick named after the physicist Richard Feynman. Explain a thing in words a kid could follow. Wherever you get stuck, that's the part you don't really understand yet.

So I tried it on AI, starting with the part that happens every time you hit send. It's called inference. (A fancy word for "using the thing.")

My first try went like this. Your words get turned into numbers. The AI searches through everything it learned from and finds what an answer should look like.

The second half of that is wrong, and I only found out by trying to explain it. Which is the whole point of the trick.

## The gap in my answer

What I was missing is that an AI has two completely separate lives.

<StudyVsExam caption="Studying and the exam never happen at the same time. By the time you're asking questions, the studying is long over." />

Studying is called **training**. The AI reads an enormous pile of text (books, websites, code) and plays a guessing game with it, over and over. Hide the next word, guess it, check, adjust. Every wrong guess nudges billions of tiny dials inside it. When training is done, the AI doesn't keep a copy of the text. What's left is the dials.

Then comes a shorter second round. People write example conversations and rate the AI's answers, and the dials get nudged toward the helpful ones. That's what turns a text finisher into an assistant you can chat with.

### What a dial actually does

A dial is just a number that sets how hard one thing pushes toward another. Here's a tiny model with nine of them. Each clue word on the left pushes on each answer across the top, and the pushes add up into percentages. Turn a dial and watch the guess change, or press Study and watch training do it.

Watch the night sentence. "Sky" still pushes toward blue, so "night" has to push toward black even harder. That's dials working together. And when studying is done, none of the sentences is stored anywhere. Only the nine settings are.

A real model works the same way with billions of dials. They're the **parameters** (or **weights**) you hear about in the news, and nobody sets them by hand.

The exam is **inference**. That's you asking a question, and it's closed-book. The AI can't flip back through what it read, because it doesn't have it. All it has is the dials, set exactly where studying left them. It keeps what the books taught it, not the books. (Kind of like how you can't recite your high school chemistry textbook, but you still know not to mix bleach and ammonia. It can memorize a few bits it saw many, many times, like a song chorus, but there's no library to search.)

Here's what happens on the exam, in four steps. Your words run through the dials in almost every one of them.

## First, chop it into tokens

The AI can't read letters. So your question gets chopped into small pieces called **tokens**. Common words usually stay whole. Long or rare words get split into chunks the AI has seen plenty of times.

<TokenSplit
  text="Painting happy little trees is unbelievably relaxing."
  caption="Type your own sentence. The ␣ is a space, which rides along with the next word. This is a simplified splitter to show the idea, and the numbers are made up. Real ones pick their chunks by how often they show up in the training text, so their splits are often weirder than this. On average a token is about three quarters of an English word."
/>

Each token is swapped for an ID number. From here on, the AI only sees numbers.

## Second, give every token a spot on a map

An ID number says nothing about meaning. So every token also gets a spot on a giant map, where words with similar meanings sit close together.

<WordMap
  words={story.map}
  pick="puppy"
  caption="A hand-drawn version with two directions (left to right, top to bottom). A real model's map has thousands of directions. That's impossible to draw, so this is the sketch."
/>

Nobody draws this map by hand. Each word's spot is more dials, set during studying. Words used in similar ways keep getting nudged together until they end up as neighbors. (A word's spot on the map is the "vector" people talk about. It's just the word's address.)

Notice "bank," stuck by itself between the river and the money. That's the next problem.

## Third, let the words look at each other

"Bank" means two different things, and on its own the AI can't tell which one you meant. So the next step lets every word look back at the words before it and work out which ones matter to it. This is called **attention**.

<AttentionDemo
  focus="bank"
  ends={['the edge of a river', 'a place for money']}
  sentences={story.attention}
  caption="Switch sentences and watch what 'bank' pays attention to. Thicker arcs matter more. The last one is a toss-up even for a person. The numbers are made up to show the idea."
/>

The AI does this dozens of times in a row. Each round is called a **layer**. In each one, every word looks back, then its spot gets adjusted by running through more dials. With every layer, the spots shift a little more based on what's around them. By the end, "bank" in a sentence about fishing means something different from "bank" in a sentence about a paycheck. (Same goes for "it," "they," and every other word that depends on what came before.)

## Last, guess the next token and do it all again

After all those layers, the AI does one thing. It has a fixed list of every token it knows (more than a hundred thousand in newer models), and it uses the dials to give each one a chance of coming next. Those are the percentages you've been seeing in the bars.

Then it picks one. Usually the top one, but not always. A little randomness keeps it from sounding like a robot, and it's why asking the same question twice can get you two different answers.

The picked token goes on the end, and the whole thing runs again with the answer so far included. Every word in an AI's answer is one trip around this loop.

## So is it just autocomplete?

Your phone's keyboard guesses your next word too. The part my first try got right is that the AI isn't looking up an answer either. It's predicting what an answer would look like.

That's also why AI can be confidently wrong. If a made-up fact looks like the kind of thing that comes next, it comes out just as smoothly as a real one. Nothing in the loop checks it against the books, because there are no books.

But the "just" in "just autocomplete" is doing a lot of work. To guess the next word of a joke, a recipe, or a math proof really well, the dials have to capture a surprising amount about how the world works. Same idea as your keyboard. A wildly better guesser.

## What's next

You may have seen ChatGPT say it's searching the web, or read a file you gave it, or run some code. Guessing words can't do any of that. Those are **tools**, the next big step chatbots took. The model writes a request, a regular program does the work, and the result gets pasted into the conversation before the guessing goes on. That's the next post. After that come agents, which run this loop over and over, with tools, to get real work done.

This post is about 1,250 words, so a model would need roughly 1,700 guesses to write it. I needed a lot more than that.
