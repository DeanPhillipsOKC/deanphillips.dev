This is part 10 of a series about Gesso, an ongoing experiment in turning any picture into a painting lesson. The [overview](/blog/gesso/) has the story so far, and [part 9](/blog/gesso-hands-off/) ended with a promise to give the tool real outlines instead of boxes. This is that.

## The lessons weren't detailed enough

I had three complaints about my own tool. The lessons didn't tell a new painter enough. Too much of each lesson came from an AI guessing when plain math would do. And every step was a fresh picture from the image model, which repaints the whole canvas each time, so most of a run went to repairing what it had moved.

My fix was to take the AI out of everywhere it didn't need to be. In my words at the time, "if we could do more stuff without inference it would be faster and we would get more consistent results." That turned a small experiment in the backlog into the main bet. Paint the finished picture once, then cut every step out of it.

I set the rules first. More detail for a new painter. A free tool wherever one gives a good result. No paying for a fancier image setting. Smaller steps (12 to 24, up from 5 to 12). And never drop a color a painter can see.

The first try cut the steps with boxes and fell over on the lavender sunset, where the sky's gold and the field's gold match. A box can't tell where one stops. So I asked for real outlines. The agent tested a free outline model, wrote a design inside my rules, two fresh reviewers picked it apart, and I approved it before anything was built.

Nothing in that stack was drawn separately. Because every step comes from the same painting, a road can't change shape between steps and a bird can't grow a different head. The reviews and repairs that existed to catch that are gone too.

## How a computer finds the parts

You see a bird. A computer sees a grid of colored dots. Getting from dots to "this is the bird" takes two tricks, because paintings have two kinds of things in them.

**Things with an edge** (a bird, a sail, a hill) get a box. The agent measures roughly where the thing is, and a small model that has learned what edges look like traces the outline inside it.

**Things without an edge** (a branch that wanders across the whole picture, mist, a reflection) defeat that model completely. The first try at the branch came back as most of the background. What works is almost embarrassingly simple. Point at a few spots that are the branch and a few that aren't. Then ask every dot, is your color closer to the yes spots or the no spots? That's the whole rule. Press Clear above to place your own, and watch one more tap fix what goes wrong.

## Under the hood

The outline model is Segment Anything, an open model from Meta, in its smallest version. It's squeezed down to 108 MB and runs with ONNX Runtime on the CPU. No graphics card, no PyTorch, no cloud. The rest is Python, NumPy, and OpenCV. The color rule compares colors in Lab, a color space built so distances roughly match what your eye sees, and it reads each spot as the middle color of a small patch so one stray brush mark can't decide it.

The agent's job is the hints. It lists the parts back to front and gives each one a kind (an area, an object, marks painted on something, or a thin line) and its boxes, spots, or lines, measured off a grid laid over the painting. Then each part claims its pixels, and when two want the same one, the part painted later wins. These are the real hints and the real map from a hands-off run.

The agent looks at a preview once and gets two rounds to fix a part. The same hints give the same outlines, byte for byte, on one machine. The model file is pinned by its checksum, so a changed download gets refused instead of quietly moving every outline.

## Small steps cost nothing now

When each step was its own picture, splitting one meant another render and hoping it matched. So steps stayed big. The old chickadee lesson took the bird from a flat tan shape to finished feathers in one step.

Now a step is a different cut of the same painting, so it can be as small as a beginner needs. A flat coat first, then one pass per paint, where each pass shows the finish wherever its color is nearest that paint.

## Who does what

I make the decisions, the agents do the typing, and nothing ships until I've looked at it. The direction, the rules, the version number, and every merge were mine. The agents built it in eight issues, each reviewed before it reached me. Then three different models each made the same three lessons with nobody helping, and all nine finished. The smallest was the fastest and planned the worst (it painted every sky as ragged patches, so there's a rule against that now). The new version went live when I scored the lessons and said "The pics look good."

## What broke

Plenty.

- **The first outlines were stretched.** Every part sat a little below where it belonged. The model works on a padded 1,024 pixel square, and its full-size output assumes the picture was square too. A second reviewer caught it by looking. Now the tool reads the model's 256 pixel answer and cuts off the padding itself, and a test checks outlines against saved ones.
- **A ghost boat.** The sky runs straight through where the boat will go, but the finish doesn't show that sky. The first fills left a blurry boat in every early step. Now one extra picture, the painting with the boat taken out, fills in behind it.
- **The red sailboat with no red paint.** I'd asked for at most ten paint mixes in a lesson. The tool obeyed by merging the hull's red into a muddy brown. The rule is now "aim for ten, but never merge two colors a painter could tell apart."
- **Soil on a blank canvas.** Bits of path between lavender rows got filed under "sky" and painted eleven steps early. Fixed. The lesson made before the fix says so on its page.
- **It isn't much faster yet.** Fewer pictures cut the image bill to about a sixth, but they didn't buy as much time as I hoped. Most of a run is the agent planning and checking, and that's where the minutes are.
