This is part 10 of a series about Gesso, an ongoing experiment in turning any picture into a painting lesson. The [overview](/blog/gesso/) has the story so far, and [part 9](/blog/gesso-hands-off/) ended with a promise to give the tool real outlines instead of boxes. This is that.

## The lessons weren't detailed enough

I had three complaints about my own tool. The lessons didn't tell a new painter enough. Too much of each lesson came from an AI guessing when plain math would do. And every step was a fresh picture from the image model, which repaints the whole canvas each time, so most of a run went to repairing what it had moved.

So I asked the strongest model I have to take the whole thing apart and tell me what it would do instead. Its answer was one sentence long. Paint the finished picture once, then cut every step out of it.

Nothing in that stack was drawn separately. It is one picture with its parts found and lifted out. Because every step comes from the same painting, a road can't change shape between steps and a bird can't grow a different head. That whole class of problem is gone, along with the reviews and repairs that existed to catch it.

## How a computer finds the parts

You look at a painting of a bird and see a bird. A computer sees a grid of colored dots. Getting from dots to "this is the bird" took two different tricks, because paintings have two kinds of things in them.

**Things with an edge** (a bird, a sail, a hill) get a box. The agent measures roughly where the thing is, and a small model that has learned what edges look like traces the outline inside the box. It runs on an ordinary computer in about ten seconds, with no image model involved.

**Things without an edge** (a branch that wanders across the whole picture, mist, a reflection) defeat that model completely. My first try at the branch came back as most of the background. What works is almost embarrassingly simple. Point at a few spots that are the branch and a few that aren't. Then ask of every dot in the picture, is your color closer to the yes spots or the no spots? That's the whole rule. Try it above and you'll see where it goes wrong and how one more tap fixes it.

## Small steps cost nothing now

When each step was its own picture from the image model, splitting a step in two meant asking for another one and hoping it matched. So steps stayed big. The old chickadee lesson took the bird from a flat tan shape to finished feathers in a single step, which is a lot to ask of someone who has painted ten pictures.

Now a step is just a different cut of the same painting, so it can be as small as a beginner needs. A flat coat first, then one pass per paint.

The same outlines get used again on the lesson page. Each step now shows where its paint goes, a close-up when the area is small, and a map of which paint goes where. None of it is the AI describing a picture. It's measured.

## Who did the work

I made the decisions. One model wrote the design and built the risky first piece. It handed the rest to other agents one issue at a time and reviewed what came back before I saw it. Then I had three different models each make the same three lessons with nobody helping. All nine finished on their own. The smallest model was the fastest and planned the worst (it painted every sky as ragged patches, so there's a rule against that now).

## What broke

Plenty.

- **The first outlines were stretched.** Every part sat a little below where it belonged, and I'd already written down that it worked. A second reviewer caught it by looking at the picture.
- **A ghost boat.** The sky is painted straight through where the boat will go, and the finished painting doesn't show that sky. The first fills left a blurry boat shape in every step before the boat. Now one extra picture, the painting with the boat taken out, fills in what's behind it.
- **The red sailboat with no red paint.** I'd asked for at most ten paint mixes in a lesson. The tool obeyed by merging the hull's red into a muddy brown. The rule is now "aim for ten, but never merge two colors a painter could tell apart."
- **Soil on a blank canvas.** Bits of the path between lavender rows got filed under "sky" and painted eleven steps early. Fixed in the tool. The lesson made before the fix still shows it, and it says so on its page.
- **It isn't much faster yet.** Fewer pictures cut the image bill to about a sixth, but they didn't buy as much time as I hoped. Most of a run is the agent planning and checking, and that's where the minutes are.
