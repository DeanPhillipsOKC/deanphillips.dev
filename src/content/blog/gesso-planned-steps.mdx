This is part 2 of a series about Gesso, an ongoing experiment in turning any picture into a painting lesson. This part covers day one. The [overview](/blog/gesso/) has the finished lessons to flip through.

## The painting-class steps had to go

After [part 1](/blog/gesso-sky-first/), the lessons went in the right order, but they still followed a painting class: tone the canvas, sketch, block in shapes, build up. I don't do any of that when I follow a tutorial. Neither do the tutorials. They hand you a white canvas and say "load up your big brush, we're starting with the sky."

So I added a second style, **paint-along**, written the way those videos go: sky, far-away shapes, ground, the main subject, texture, shadows, highlights. Seven steps, same order for every picture.

It worked well on the aurora picture, which has all seven of those things. Then it got to the trees.

This picture has no ground, so the "paint the ground" step repainted the sky with new clouds. These trees have no leaves, so "add leaves and texture," "shadows," and "highlights" came out as three nearly identical pictures. A beginner following along would wonder what they were supposed to be doing.

## Letting Claude plan the steps

The fix was to stop deciding the steps ahead of time. Now Claude looks at the finished painting, lists its parts from back to front (sunset sky, clouds, the sun's glow, trunks, branches), and makes a step for each part or small group. The trees got seven steps, and every one of them adds something you can see. The aurora got eight. The flying couple got nine, including one step for each of the two people.

I didn't hand over the whole lesson, though. The painting style still sets the rules, and Claude plans inside them:

- Paint back to front, and paint background straight through where thin things like branches will go.
- One part per step. No empty steps, and no step for something the picture doesn't have.
- Big shapes before small ones. Texture and highlights only where the picture has them.
- Every color is a mix of the same five tubes of paint, with a name you can remember.

That last rule turned into my favorite part. Flip back to the second tab above: each step lists its paints with a swatch and a recipe, like "Sunset Orange: 3 parts yellow, 1 part red, 1 part white." When a paint comes back in a later step, it has the same name, so you know to use what's still on your palette.

## What broke

The swatches. Claude is good at naming a color and writing a recipe for it, but it was guessing the swatch color from memory. So I had it point to a spot in the painting where each paint shows up, and the program read the real color from that spot.

<Callout title="It pointed at the wrong spots.">"Golden Glow," the warm yellow around the sun, came out dark brown, because the spot it picked was on a tree trunk. "Aurora Green" came out plain blue sky.</Callout>

The version that stuck uses both. It starts from Claude's color, looks near the spot for the closest color actually in the painting, and moves the swatch only a little. Two different paints are never allowed to end up as the same swatch.
