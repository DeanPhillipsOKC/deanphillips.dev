This is part 1 of a series about Gesso, an ongoing experiment in turning any picture into a painting lesson. This part covers day one. The [overview](/blog/gesso/) has the finished lessons to flip through.

## Version one: darks first

My first try copied the method painting classes teach: tone the canvas with a thin orange wash, sketch the shapes, paint the darkest areas, then fill in everything else and add detail. Flip through it, then switch to the other tab.

Step 3 of version one is where it goes wrong. The trees are done and the sky doesn't exist yet. To finish the painting, you'd have to fill in the sky around hundreds of branches with a tiny brush. Every tutorial I've followed does it the other way around.

So I asked: wouldn't it make more sense to do the sky and then the trees?

It would. Acrylic dries fast and covers well, so you paint what's farthest away first and put each closer thing on top. The sky goes on in big, easy strokes, right across where the trees will be. Then the trunks go over it. If a branch comes out wobbly, it doesn't matter, because there's sky underneath.

That became the first rule Gesso follows for every lesson: **paint back to front.**

## What broke on the way

Getting the computer to actually do that took a few tries. An AI follows directions very literally, and these are the times it followed mine a little too well.

The pattern in all of these is the same. The image model never sees the lesson. It sees one request at a time, so anything I don't spell out, it makes up fresh. Most of the later work, which the rest of this series covers, is about spelling things out: what's already on the canvas, what isn't there yet, and what has to stay exactly where it is.
