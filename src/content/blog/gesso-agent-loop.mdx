This is part 6 of a series about Gesso, an ongoing experiment in turning any picture into a painting lesson. The [overview](/blog/gesso/) has the story so far, and [part 5](/blog/gesso-filters/) covers the free filters this part leans on.

## Inside its head

Right now the agent is Claude, running inside Claude Code and following a long instruction file I wrote. This is the exploring phase: letting Claude Code run the agent loop means I can focus on whether the lessons work, without building the loop myself or paying for every thought. When Gesso becomes an app, that loop will have to live somewhere else, and that will be its own entry in this series.

The thinking is covered for now. Pictures cost real money, a couple of cents each from an image model, so the most important rule in the file is: **free tools first, and pay only when nothing free will do, with a written reason every time.**

Below is every decision it made for the lesson further down, straight from its log: 16 pictures and 52 tool choices. The agent's mind is drawn as a turning 3D space with a cluster of points for each tool. For the picture you pick, a path travels from the middle to each tool it tried, and the tool answers yes or no. Green tools are free, purple costs money, and the yellow one is a check. Click through the pictures, or drag the space to turn it. The reasons underneath are its own words.

## Too busy? Make it simpler first

The first decision is the newest rule. The first time I ran this picture, the lesson copied every detail of it: a village of tiny houses, veined clouds, and every lavender stalk. One step jumped from smooth purple rows straight to thousands of individual stalks. No beginner paints that.

So I added a check before anything else: could a beginner paint this in an afternoon? If not, make a loose, simpler version first, and build the whole lesson from that. On this picture the agent said no, wrote its own list of what to drop (the village, the ropes, the bushes), what to merge (the clouds into a few big masses), and what to turn into a repeated brush mark (the lavender into rows of dabs), and painted it for 2 cents.

## The diagrams it decided to draw

A finished step picture shows the shape, not how to get there. I noticed that on an earlier lesson, a pumpkin patch, where every pumpkin appeared fully formed with no hint of how to start one. The real tutorial I'd learned from starts with an oval and adds curved pieces on each side, and that's what made it click for me.

Instead of fixing the pumpkin, I asked for the general version: look at every step like someone who has never painted, decide where the picture isn't enough, and draw how to build the shape from simple pieces. The diagrams are drawn as code, not AI images, so they're free. On this lesson nobody told it where to look, and it picked three.
