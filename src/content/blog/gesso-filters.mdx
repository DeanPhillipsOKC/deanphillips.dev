This is part 5 of a series about Gesso, an ongoing experiment in turning any picture into a painting lesson. The [overview](/blog/gesso/) has the story so far. Part 5 picks the experiment back up three weeks later, with a new rule: **free filters first, paid AI images only when nothing free will do.**

The picture for this part is one I made for it: a hot air balloon over lavender at sunset, painted by an AI image model from a one-line description. Lots of color, a few clear shapes, and a balloon that's secretly hard to paint.

## Finding the palette

The first thing a painting lesson needs is the paints to mix. A picture has hundreds of thousands of colors, and you want six. Here's the method Gesso uses, running live on the balloon picture. Every dot on the right is one pixel, placed by its color: lighter is higher, and the hue goes around. The big circles are the paint colors it's looking for.

<KMeansPalette
  src="/img/gesso/day2/balloon-kmeans-src.webp"
  alt="A hot air balloon over a lavender field at sunset"
  caption="K-means finding six paint colors in the balloon picture. Drag the slider to ask for more or fewer, or start over from new random guesses."
/>

It's called **k-means clustering**, and the whole thing is four moves:

1. **Guess.** Drop six centers on random pixels. They're bad guesses, on purpose.
2. **Join.** Every pixel joins the center closest to its color.
3. **Move.** Each center moves to the average color of the pixels that joined it.
4. **Repeat** until the centers stop moving.

That's it. Watch the left side: every round, the picture repaints itself using only the centers' colors, and it gets a little more right. It usually settles in under ten rounds.

One detail matters a lot. "Closest color" is measured in a color space called **Lab**, built so that equal distances look equally different to a human eye. Measure in plain red, green, and blue instead, and two dark purples can count as further apart than a purple and a gray that look nothing alike.

Each center becomes a paint on the lesson's shopping list. Different random guesses can land on slightly different palettes, so the real tool cheats a little: it spreads its first guesses out on purpose (a variant called k-means++), tries four starts, and keeps the tightest result.

## Turning colors into recipes

Six colors on a screen aren't six paints yet. A beginner has five tubes: white, yellow, red, ultramarine blue, and burnt sienna. The lesson has to say how much of each to mix, and that's harder than it sounds, because paint doesn't mix like light. Pick two tubes and see:

<LightVsPaint caption="The same two tubes mixed three ways. Drag the slider to change how much of each. The paint colors come from Mixbox, a pigment mixing model by Secret Weapons." />

Light adds up. Shine a blue spotlight and a yellow one on the same wall and the overlap gets brighter, heading toward white. Paint works the other way. Each pigment soaks up part of the light, and you see what's left. Blue soaks up the reds and yellows, yellow soaks up the blues, and what neither takes much of is green. A plain average of the two screen colors gets neither right. It lands on a muddy olive.

At first, Gesso's agent wrote the recipes by eye: "3 parts white, 1 part yellow, a touch of red." They read fine. Then I checked them with Mixbox, which is fit to how real paints behave, and most were off by an amount you can see.

So now the computer works them out. For each paint, it tries every simple recipe from the five tubes (up to three colors plus white, in whole parts or "a touch"), mixes each one in Mixbox, and keeps the one that lands closest to the color k-means found. That's a couple of thousand pretend mixes per paint, and it still takes under a second.

Those numbers are Delta E, the standard score for how different two colors look. Under 2 is hard to see, and over about 8 anyone would notice. They come from the 55 paints in five lessons Gesso has made. One balloon color, a blue-green teal, can't be mixed from these tubes at all, and the lesson now says so instead of pretending.

The other change came from painting the lessons ourselves. My girlfriend and I are beginners, and we're slow. If we mix every color at the start, the acrylics dry into crusty puddles before we need them. So every step now lists the paints it uses with their recipes, and marks the new ones, so you can mix as you go.

## Squinting, with math

Painters squint at their subject to see **values**: how light or dark things are, with the color taken away. It's the most important thing in a painting and the easiest to get wrong.

A value study is the same k-means trick, run on lightness alone. Slide through it:

<Flipbook sequences={[values]} caption="The balloon picture split into 2, 3, 4, and 5 values. Each value is one shade of gray." />

Look at the sky. At three values, that whole blazing sunset is **one value**, the lightest. Every orange, pink, and gold in it is about the same lightness. That tells a beginner something useful: paint the sky light and keep it light, and save your darks for the lavender rows and the tree.

## Two values: the notan

Squeeze it down to just black and white and you get a **notan**, a Japanese design idea about the balance of dark and light. A picture that reads well as a notan reads well from across the room.

<img src={img('notan')} alt="The balloon picture as pure black and white" width="1200" height="800" loading="lazy" />

The hard part is where to draw the line between dark and light. Gesso uses **Otsu's method**, from 1979. It tries every possible cut and keeps the one where the dark group and the light group are each as tight as they can be. No guessing, no slider.

## Simplifying without smearing

A beginner can't paint every lavender stalk. The reference has to merge small details into shapes you could paint in an afternoon. Here are two filters that do that, and they fail in different ways. Drag the divider:

<CompareSlider
  before={{ src: img('kuwahara'), label: 'Kuwahara' }}
  after={{ src: img('meanshift'), label: 'Mean shift' }}
  width={1200}
  height={800}
  caption="The same picture simplified two ways. Kuwahara makes soft paint-like patches. Mean shift keeps hard edges."
/>

- **Kuwahara** looks at four little boxes around each pixel and leans on the average of whichever box is calmest. That turns texture into flat, brushy patches, which is lovely for skies and fields. It also breaks thin lines apart. On a cartoon cat, it erased the cat's face.
- **Mean shift** lets every pixel drift toward the crowd of similar colors near it. Flat areas flatten, but a dark line stays a dark line. Gesso now tries it first on anything with outlines.
